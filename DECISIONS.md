# Decisions made without you

One line each, newest at the bottom.

- Kenney packs downloaded fine, so all art is Kenney CC0 plus generated pieces in the same style.
- Mini Characters chosen over Blocky Characters: they share Mini Market's exact colormap and proportions, and ship rigged walk/idle/pick-up/holding clips.
- World unit = one Mini Market tile; other packs are rescaled to it (see docs/STYLE_GUIDE.md) rather than the other way round, because the store is what the camera frames.
- Starter shop is 5x4 tiles plus a 5x2 storeroom: small enough that one shelf, one fridge and a register do not leave a big empty floor.
- Playwright pins `executablePath` to the preinstalled Chromium with SwiftShader so screenshots and perf run headless in CI containers without a GPU.
- Sim tick is 50 ms of game time; the renderer interpolates, so 4x stays smooth without a variable-step sim.
- Fredoka is bundled as a latin-only woff2 (30 KB) plus the full variable TTF in third_party for the record.
- Three.js samples the atlas with `flipY = false` and generated geometry uses glTF's v-down UV convention, so one texture serves both converted models and generated pieces.
- Quantised GLB attributes are expanded to Float32 at load; merging and transforming interleaved or normalised attributes was the source of the "missing neighbourhood" and "exploded people" bugs.
- Character variety is done by shifting colormap strips per triangle at load (outfit permutations, three skin tones), not by extra textures: 13 meshes become 52 looks for free.
- The shop name lives on a free-standing pylon sign by the sidewalk; a fascia sign over the door only shows when the camera looks at the back of the store, because a front fascia hid the whole front row of the floor from the default camera.
- Nature Kit greens are remapped to the town's grass greens at conversion (Kenney's are mint), so trees match roofs and lawns.
- Screenshot runs disable CSS animations: headless SwiftShader renders so slowly that fades were caught half-way.
- Kenney's cash register, standing fridge and end cap have their pivot on the front face; the renderer centres them in their tile with a per-type offset rather than editing the packs.
- The checkout line bends toward the shop's centre and every slot snaps to a walkable cell, so lines never run through a wall.
- Finance lives inside the Stats card and franchises inside Build, so the top bar keeps exactly the seven buttons the brief lists (plus a small Hint button for "what should I do next?").
- Staff work whole days; morale, raises and quitting carry the "imperfect staff" feeling without a shift scheduler.
- Rivals are a daily state machine (open, sale, drift, expand) issued through the same tick rather than a full agent replaying player commands; the balance harness checks they neither crush nor ignore the player.
- Franchises are a lighter daily roll with reports and alerts; their town buildings are stock shop fronts with a name board.
- Guided-player income of about $75/day in week one is the balance anchor: fixtures cost at most five days of that, the shop needs restocking daily, and rivals cost a third of trade at most.
- Shortages drop a line from an order instead of rejecting the order; the first version rejected whole orders and starved the shop.
- `cheat` commands only work in sandbox or under a test harness (`navigator.webdriver`), never in normal play.
- (Rework) The game is played in first person as the manager; the orbit camera survives only for the lobby, the plot picker and screenshots. Pointer lock with a drag-to-look fallback and touch sticks.
- The manager walks in real time whatever the clock speed (the step is divided by the time scale), so 4x speeds the day without making you sprint.
- New shops are a bare concrete room. A setup phase holds the clock until Open shop; deliveries still arrive while you set up (within 20 seconds), and Open shop needs a counter and some stock.
- The storeroom became a delivery pallet inside the back door: you carry every box by hand (E to pick up, E at a shelf to stock). Stockers still carry for you once hired.
- Walls stand outside the floor rectangle (Kenney walls are half a tile thick) and the grid blocks that ring, so fixtures can never clip into a wall.
- The shop got a flat roof and an unlit pale ceiling with light panels; the roof casts no shadow so the sun stands in for the shop lights on the floor. The cinematic camera hides it.
- The floor is one poured slab with saw-cut joints in new neutral greys added to the palette; the Kenney floor tiles read as blue checker.
- The town is built on property lots. Tests assert lots never overlap, every building fits its lot, nothing lands on the shop's plot, and fences stand on lot lines (Kenney fence panels run along x at yaw 0; the old ones were turned 90°).

- The town is merged in 44-unit chunks with shadow casters apart from flat ground pieces, and the woods are instanced per model and map quarter without shadows: about 200 draw calls and 725k triangles in the widest view.
- The logo was first the in-game manager's head; it is now a shop front (see below).
- The lobby runs a live demo shop at golden hour behind the menu with the camera drifting over Main Street; the menu is styled as shelf-edge labels under a swinging OPEN sign.
- Litter is spills on the floor; dropped baskets lying around read as a bug.

Starting cash stayed at $800; about $136 is left after the fit-out and a guided player makes about $57 a day in week one.
- People are solid around the player (you bump into shoppers, they step aside) and anyone within 0.3 of the lens is not drawn, so no head ever fills the screen.

Pointer lock is gone: the mouse is a pointer again (hover shows what a thing is, a click walks there and uses it), and a phone taps to walk or use, drags to turn and pinches to zoom.

Things you can use win over things you can only look at, so standing between a shelf and the pallet picks whatever you can actually do. A click on a shelf walks to whichever free side of it is nearest, so a shelf turned to face a wall is still stocked from the room.

How many there are follows each rival's share of the town's trade (× 0.45, so the crossroads does not drown in people), so a Cheapo sale visibly pulls walkers across the zebra. Closing time used to remove the last customers while their ids stayed in the queue, so the next morning the line waited on someone who no longer existed and nobody was served all day (a balance run went from 75 served to 0).
- Toy-town art direction: warm and saturated, never grey. The shop floor is a cream checker lino, the walls inside are banded in the shop's colour (skirting, a dark lower band, a cream rail, a pale upper band), fixtures are painted wood, cream and pale blue instead of Kenney's greys, and the light is warmer in the morning and evening. None is near a rival's colour (Cheapo yellow and red, Fresh & Fine deep green and cream, MegaMart blue and white): a test keeps every player primary at least 60 RGB steps from every rival's, so bags always tell stores apart. - Reaction bubbles (too pricey, out of stock, long queue, great deal, love this place) are sim events in a small ring buffer (`state.reactions`, 16 long), drawn as at most four sprites at once. One person cannot react twice in six seconds, and the good ones (deal, love) at most one every five seconds town-wide, so bubbles mean something.
- Props are placed by a pure function (`cornerProps`): each one takes the free lawn spot nearest its anchor, never on a path, the paving, the building or a bay (a test walks every corner and shop size). Bigger shops get more of them; opening days get bunting, rival sales get sale posters.
- Bloom only catches things meant to glow: lit windows, signs and lamps are given colours brighter than 1, above the bloom threshold, and nothing lit by the sun gets there. A pass before the bloom zeroes any NaN pixel, because one bad pixel smeared by the blur blanked whole frames. Tilt-shift fades in only past 34 units of zoom. Both are off on Low.
- Buying a closed rival's corner makes it one of your branches, not a second simulated floor. The building goes up in your colours and under your name, its shoppers walk in and out carrying your bags, and it reports every night like the branches out of town (a manager runs it by your rules, sharing your cash and reputation). A full second floor would mean a second pathfinding grid, staff split between shops, stock routed between two pallets and a camera walking between them: twice the sim's busiest code for the last goal on the ladder.
- The goal ladder follows the brief's first pass, tuned with the balance harness. Goal 4 asks for a $120 night (not $150): with a cashier and a stocker on the payroll a well-run 7×7 shop nets $100–200, and $150 left some seeds waiting a week for a lucky Saturday. Goal 5 asks for a third of the crossroads' shoppers over the last week (not 40% for seven days running): with three rivals a third makes you the busiest store, a strong 7×7 shop with the doubled lot holds 33–45%, and a daily 40% bar was decided by rival sales and Saturdays rather than by how well the shop ran. The quiet first days (fewer than two rivals trading) never count toward the week.
- Rivals' shoppers in the night card's shares and in their books come from one model on the player's scale: the town pool by customer type, the corner's traffic and mix, the same softmax share, awareness and reputation the way the player's own count, and the arrivals-per-expected factor (1.46) the player's spawner runs at. The player's side is the real count: everyone who came, less drivers turned away from a full lot.
- Rival books are small numbers on purpose: a basket of $3.60–5.40 is what a shopper spends per visit at the crossroads (the player's own takings run $2–4 per arrival), and fixed costs grow with the town, so a rival's profit tracks its share rather than the calendar. Cheapo closes in weeks 3–4 against a strong player, Fresh & Fine late in week 6 on one seed in six, MegaMart never (its branches pay $40 a day each toward the crossroads store).
- Out-selling Cheapo compares today's takings with Cheapo's modelled sales; a closed Cheapo counts as beaten.
- Rival moves and the day's events are rolled at close (into `state.tomorrow`) and applied when the day opens, from the same random streams in the same order, so no seeded test expectation moved. A save loaded at night without a plan rolls one at opening.
- Branches out of town are now a reward (the Market hall goal). Old saves that already have branches keep them and keep the unlock.
- The night card's costs are goods, rent and power, staff, and loans and shoplifting: the lines the day's profit is made of. Ads, billboards and card fees still come straight out of cash and do not count against the day's profit (as before).

Forty-day balance runs gridlocked MegaMart's double lot on busy days: cars backing out waited for the aisle, and the cars in the aisle waited for them.
- The balance harness's guided player now plays the way the goals and tips teach over forty days: it reorders when the pallet runs low (the truck is there in a minute and a half), orders new lines for empty shelves, moves up to the 7×7 shop when it can keep $200 for stock, doubles the lot once drivers are turned away, then hires a stocker. Its week-one profit went from ≈ $63 to ≈ $94 a day, past the brief's ≈ $75. The other strategies play twelve days (no rival may close before day 12), with going bust judged at day eight as before. Games run in parallel processes (about four minutes on four cores).
- Rivals answer the player with one move at a time from a short list per kind (Cheapo price-matches; Fresh & Fine renovates, holds a tasting day or starts a loyalty card; MegaMart runs a mega sale, buys a billboard or stays open late). Moves are bonuses on the rival's existing scores, so the scoring weights and the softmax did not change, and they fade over their last three days instead of switching off. At most one rival moves per day across the crossroads, each rests four days between its own moves and every move has its own cooldown: a town where all three answer you on the same morning reads as noise, not as rivals watching you.
- The rivals read the player through three numbers kept on each closed day (`mine`: price index, share of each customer type, reputation) rather than through the live state, so a move always follows something the player did over several days, and the night before it a rumour says it is coming.
- Cheapo's price match is 5% off its prices and 4 points deeper sales for a week: against a player 20% under the town it closes Cheapo's price gap over the player from 6–7 points to 2 (the balance harness's undercutter), so undercutting buys a few days, not a permanent edge, and still leaves the player cheaper.
- Fresh & Fine's renovation really closes it for two days (its shoppers spread to the other stores), then it reopens better for two weeks. Its 24-day cooldown keeps it to roughly once a month.
- MegaMart's billboard takes a free site the player is not renting, and the player cannot rent it while MegaMart holds it. It never takes a board the player already has.
- Move banners stand on two poles at the front of the rival's apron, facing the road, where the "Coming soon" board stood. Hung on the storefront they hid under the awning from every camera angle.

Keeping keys rather than sentences keeps saves small and lets the panel word them for the type ("Always has my brands" for a loyalist, "Always has what I need" for anyone else).
- Picking people is a screen-space test against the box each figure covers (never smaller than a fingertip), not a ray against skinned meshes: skinned bounds move with the bones, and a tap on a phone has to hit someone a few pixels wide from the overview. The nearest to the camera wins.
- The selection outline is an inverted hull: the person's skinned mesh drawn again on their skeleton, pushed out along the normals, back faces only, in the highlight yellow. It exists only while someone is selected, and the panel's data is rebuilt ten times a second only then, so selection costs nothing the rest of the time.

- "Who shops where" counts the player's shoppers as they set out today and the rivals' from the same daily model as the night card, scaled by how much of the day has gone, so the columns compare like with like.

- Cars arriving in a lot give way to cars leaving it when their next few metres cross, unless they are nose to nose (then the jam breaker sorts them out). Two cars used to meet in the aisle and overlap.

A left-drag grabs the ground and pans (one finger on a phone; two fingers twist to turn). Walking again, by key or click, brings the camera back to the manager.

Braking stays firm enough that the gap rules still stop a car in time (the rush-hour no-overlap tests pass).

Every corner, rivals included, has a garden from one pure function: fifteen tree kinds at different sizes (darker and autumn ones among them), bushes, flowers and rocks, never on paving or a prop. More trees stand in clumps round the corners' outer edges, but nothing past the edge of town, as decided for the crossroads.
- The game autosaves every 30 seconds of play, after picking a plot, and when the page is hidden or closed, as well as each night. Before, only nights were saved, so leaving during the first day left nothing to continue.
- You design your manager in the lobby, as asked: thirteen characters, skin, hair, top and trousers, height. It is a player setting, not part of a save, so every shop you start or continue wears it. Colours are painted per body part from a measured table of where each character keeps its skin and hair in the colour map (the strips differ between characters; a shopper's skin shift would turn one character's hair skin-coloured), and every swatch is a colour in the baked palette so it shows exactly.
- The manager designer starts with Guy or Girl, six characters each. The shop-worker character (in the shop's uniform) is left out of the choice: the manager wears their own clothes.
- Surface detail is one shader addition on the shared world material, not textures per model: Kenney's models colour every face from one atlas swatch, so a texture per surface would mean UVs and art per model. Sampling a noise texture in world space along each surface's main axis works on every model, keeps the instancing and merged geometry, and costs three texture reads a pixel (blending all three axes cost eight and slowed the software-rendered test browser by half again; the town is boxes, so the blend bought nothing visible). What a surface is (concrete, asphalt, roofing, paint) is read from its colour, so the same grey reads the same everywhere.
- Car easing cost junction throughput: pulling away at 3.2 units/s² passed fewer cars per green, and on the busiest days of a forty-day game the queue grew past the edge of town and fed itself (over 400 cars, and the balance run five times slower). Pulling away at 5 keeps the ease visible, braking for a bend is now only for cars turning off the road, and through traffic and turned-away cars (only for show) are not spawned into a queue at the edge of town.
- Every model, texture and data fetch carries the build id (`?v=`), so the static assets can be cached as immutable and a deploy can never be served the last one's files from the browser cache; the HTML itself must revalidate. CI gives tests five minutes (the screenshot run takes over two on its runners).
- Slowing for a queue or a red light follows a square-root profile (constant braking) instead of a straight line in the gap: from road speed the line asked for 16–20 units/s² of braking, more than the 12 a car may use once speeds are eased, so cars overshot into the gap and snapped to a stop, and one ran a red in the rush-hour test. A stop line that holds a car is also a hard limit on how far it may move.
- A shelf's stand spot (front, else back or a side) must be reachable from the front door by the walking grid. Reachable from somewhere was not enough: in a forty-day run the manager was sent to the free tile behind a fridge that two shelves and the back wall had sealed off, walked through the fridge to get there (no path, so straight) and stood there all day, rescued twenty-four times.
- The e2e hook that reports the camera settled first re-reads the panels on screen. In the test browser (software GL, about two frames a second) a card that had just opened had not yet slid the view aside when a test aimed at a tile, so the ghost moved off the floor under a still mouse.
- Stocking fills every slot of the product that has room and then free slots, instead of only the first slot already holding it: a full slot used to turn a whole box away beside three empty ones ("That one is full"). The message now says which it is (really full, or every spot holding other products) and the prompt over a shelf says how many will go on.
- Backing out follows a precomputed arc on the rear axle (straight 0.8, then a 1.35 radius, about a real car's at this scale), position and heading both read off it, instead of sliding straight back while the heading turned on its own. Bays are 1.1 apart and cars 0.82 wide, so the straight part is what keeps the nose off the neighbours (a test backs out of every bay of every lot, one and two rows, and checks the rear axle never slides sideways, the heading turns no faster than the arc, and the body never touches the cars either side).
- A car turning across the oncoming lane waits for a gap of about 14 units (2.3 seconds at road speed), up from 10, and keeps checking until its nose is in that lane: turning cars slow for the bend, so they take longer to clear it. A strict no-overlap rule for moving cars and a both-must-be-waiting deadlock rule were tried and dropped: at junctions cars graze corners under the normal rules, and both turned brief touches into long jams.
- The trees are generated rather than picked from Kenney's other tree models: Kenney's are 50–400 triangles of one or two blobs, and the more detailed ones are pines. Generated in the same faceted style and palette, under the ids the gardens already use, they cost fewer triangles in all than the mix they replace (140k at the default zoom, down from 158k), and their GLBs are no longer fetched.
- A click on furniture with empty hands opens a menu (its use, Move, Turn) rather than picking the piece straight up: the same click already opened Stock on a shelf (asked for earlier) and took you behind the counter to scan, the till being the piece clicked most. The menu keeps both a click away and makes moving one more, without the Build card. Carrying a box skips the menu (the click stocks the shelf), and F still does a piece's job directly.
- The night card can be closed and is read again from Stats: each night's goal progress and forecast are stored with that day's stats (the rest of a day's report already was), so an old day's log shows what it said that night. A card opened at night takes the log's place until it is closed, rather than opening behind it, and a complaint's card button says which day it opens on when it is still locked, as the tool shelf does.
- Cut down to a ledge, a window wall piece is drawn as a plain wall piece: squashing the window's frame and pane down to the ledge left faces of different colours fighting on the sill. Inside, the paint stops at the window's recessed frame (Kenney paints window glass in the atlas, so there was no pane to cut around and the paint covered the windows).
- A shopper at a shelf looks, then reaches once with the left hand as the item is taken (timed to the sim at any game speed); at the till one reach to put things down (one per item at a self-checkout), then standing. Looping Kenney's 0.33 s pick-up clip made shoppers bob three times a second; looping the interact clip made the till twitch. Before paying, shoppers carry a red basket in the right hand: they used to walk the aisles holding nothing in a two-armed carry pose.
- Dense grass is a ring of instanced patches round the camera's focus with a top-down coverage mask, not more scattered tufts: tufts merged over the whole town at the density asked for would be millions of triangles and tens of megabytes, while the ring is one draw of about 130k triangles wherever you look. The mask is drawn from the scene itself, so the grass agrees with every corner's lot, paths and buildings without a second copy of their rules.
- Trees made by us carry an index like every Kenney model: a non-indexed tree made each 44-unit town chunk it stood in fail to merge, and those chunks were not drawn (found when the grass work logged it).
- No environment map: lighting everything with the sky's prefiltered map took a frame in the software-rendered test browser from about 0.55 s to 1.1 s (several texture reads a pixel on every material) and timed tests out; modest GPUs pay the same. The hemisphere and ambient lights carry the fill, and puddles work the sky's colour out in the shader from shared uniforms.
- Weather follows the forecast the night card gave (heavy rain may become a thunderstorm, grey may drizzle) using a hash of seed and day, never a random stream, so trade and balance roll exactly as before; within the day it is a pure function of time, and only the day's kind is saved. Lightning strikes on a real-time clock (slots of 0.8 s), so a storm keeps its pace at any game speed.
- Rain on the ground follows what most games do cheaply: wetness darkens and smooths up-facing outdoor surfaces, puddles come from a broad, smooth noise (a fine one made the road glitter), and in them the sky is reflected with Fresnel and broken by procedural ripple rings (four cells a pixel, no textures). Screen-space or planar reflections of buildings were left out: both need the scene drawn again each frame.
- Rain stops on roofs, awnings and benches, and splashes land on top of them, from the height the grass mask already draws from above; it costs nothing extra a frame.
- A fender bender holds the two cars for six to eight minutes of game time, not half an hour: cars queued behind start to creep after eight seconds held up (the jam breaker), and a longer bump let them creep up to the stopped car. Making the queue wait patiently behind a held car instead upset the junction (a red light was run in the crossroads test).
- Town life draws on its own random stream and nothing in it is the player's doing: only mess on the shop's frontage touches the game (a little cleanliness, a face pulled, occasionally a "messy" complaint), and it can be picked up or waits for the overnight sweep.
- Brighter by grading, not by changing every colour: exposure 1.16, a stronger sky fill, and one pass after tone mapping (vibrance weighted to the duller colours, a little warmth, a gentle lift of the mid-tones, a vignette half as strong). Asphalt is lifted in the surface-detail shader and the wall pieces' charcoal is mapped to slate, since those two were the darkest large areas on screen. Days with nothing forecast are now mostly fair or clear, and the first two always are; the forecast's own grey and rain days are unchanged.
- The clock's speed and the sim's time scale are reset together (at night and on load). The manager's pace is divided by the time scale so they walk in real time at any speed; resetting only the clock left them at a quarter pace.
- At night only the build tools take clicks (place, move, turn, the Build card's moves). Everything else stays off, since the shop is shut.
- The listing is on a made-up map app, Pinpoint, in a map app's familiar layout. Using a real company's name, logo or colours in the game was avoided.
- Review photos are rendered from the live scene and kept in memory only. Saving them would add tens of kilobytes each to every save; a loaded game retakes them from the town as it stands, so an old review's photo can show today's shop. A photo costs about a frame and is only taken between frames (one a frame while the profile is open, otherwise one every few seconds).
- Reviews draw on their own random stream, but they are not cosmetic: once a shop has five reviews the rating pulls a quarter of the nightly reputation drift. How the shop looks counts for as much as the stock in a review, so decor now matters to trade.
- Shoppers post reviews when they get home (20 to 200 game minutes later, the rest at night), and the badge counts every review the shop has, as asked. The new ones since the profile was last open turn it red.
